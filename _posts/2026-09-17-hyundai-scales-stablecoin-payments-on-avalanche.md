Hyundai Card is scaling its stablecoin payment test on the Avalanche network after a live corporate transfer settled in about seven minutes. On September 16, Avalanche said the South Korean payments unit's next job is proving the operating model works at higher volume, the step between a one-off pilot and routine treasury plumbing.

## What happened in the July pilot

On July 9, Hyundai Card announced it had finished its first proof of concept: a real intercompany payment between Hyundai Motor America and Hyundai Motor Mexico. Hyundai Motor America converted $20,000 into Tether's USDT, sent the tokens across Avalanche, and the Mexican unit converted them back to dollars on arrival. Hyundai Card says the full run, remittance plus verification, averaged about seven minutes. A comparable interbank transfer would take three to four hours, sometimes longer.

The sum was small by corporate standards. The point was that it was real money, moving through accounting, tax, and control reviews in two countries. Ava Labs says that makes Hyundai the first major enterprise to publicly put this kind of live treasury settlement on Avalanche.

Hyundai Card is the payments arm of Hyundai Motor Group, one of the world's largest carmakers. It ran the pilot with Tether, which supplied USDT, Avalanche for the chain, and Axiym, a Swiss-based payments infrastructure firm Tether has invested in. Hyundai Card handled the compliance side itself: regulatory review, accounting treatment, tax checks, internal controls, and the structure of the remittance flow.

## What does "works at scale" mean?

A stablecoin is a digital token pegged to a traditional currency, in this case the dollar, so its value holds near 1:1 instead of swinging like Bitcoin does.

A single $20,000 transfer proved the mechanics. It did not test repeated volume, larger sums, or several jurisdictions running without someone watching every step. When Avalanche shared the next stage on September 16, it quoted Heejung Nam, head of payments and business development at Hyundai Card: "We have to prove the entire operation model works at scale." She added, "What happens if we can scale up? Then the economic model works."

That last line is the real test. Moving one transfer in seven minutes is one thing. Running a treasury on stablecoins daily also means managing liquidity, conversion spreads, and the staff time spent when a transfer stalls. Hyundai Card has not published a target volume, a bigger test amount, or the performance thresholds it needs to hit before it commits. Nor has it shared a cost comparison for a larger rollout.

## Is Europe still next?

Hyundai Card planned a second proof of concept for late July across its European subsidiaries, using currencies other than the dollar. Circle, the issuer of USDC, and Visa were set to join, and the goal was to measure foreign-exchange costs, not just speed.

As of mid-September, Hyundai Card has not publicly confirmed that European run is done. The latest update keeps coming back to scale on the tested US-Mexico corridor rather than reporting results from a currency-diverse one.

## Why does a carmaker moving $20,000 matter?

Corporate blockchain history is littered with demos that never carried a real invoice. Hyundai's pilot is the opposite: two subsidiaries, two countries, one live dollars-to-USDT-to-dollars loop that an auditor could sign.

The group that would scale it is one of the biggest manufacturers on earth. If the rails hold at volume, other multinationals get a worked template, including the compliance paperwork. Avalanche has been courting exactly this business. Its Payments Collective, a network of payment firms, banks, and asset managers, includes Axiym and has been pushing cross-border settlement infrastructure.

Korea's future is the wildcard. Amended foreign-exchange rules passed in May take effect in late 2026 and will require virtual-asset transfer businesses to register and report cross-border data. Whether internal intercompany stablecoin moves count under that test depends on an implementing decree still being written. A broader Digital Asset Basic Act that would formally recognize stablecoins for corporate payments is stalled, with the Bank of Korea and the Financial Services Commission split over who can issue won-pegged stablecoins.

## What to watch next

- A bigger notional. The first test was $20,000; watch for a larger sum or a completed transfer count.
- The Europe phase. Any confirmation that the Circle-Visa pilot ran, and its foreign-exchange numbers.
- The economics. A published cost-per-transfer comparison against the bank route.
- Korea's clock. The foreign-exchange amendments land in late 2026; the stablecoin law is stuck.

## Bottom line

Hyundai Card cleared the technical hurdle with a live payment that took seven minutes. The economic hurdle is still ahead: whether the system handles repeated, larger, multi-country treasury volume without the overhead it replaced. Korea's regulators also have to make room at home. A commercial rollout date is unset, so the next test result will say more than any announcement.
