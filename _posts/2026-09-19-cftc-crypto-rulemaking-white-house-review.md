Two days after the Senate blocked the CLARITY Act, the CFTC sent its crypto market structure rulemaking to the White House for review. The Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA), the office that vets federal rules before they reach the public, logged the derivatives regulator's filing on September 17 under the title "Regulation Crypto Asset Transactions and Regulation Crypto Asset Markets," identification number RIN 3038-AF80.

For now, this is the closest thing the US has to a crypto regulatory timeline, and the agency is driving it.

## What exactly did the CFTC file?

A prerule. That's the earliest formal step in US federal rulemaking, the point where an agency signals intent before it writes an actual proposal.

The filing is thin on detail. The OIRA entry carries the RIN, a title, and a flag that it falls under the Dodd-Frank Act. It lists no legal deadline, marks the action as not economically significant, and includes no public text. Crypto reporter Eleanor Terrett flagged the entry on X, noting it landed two days after CFTC Chairman Michael Selig said the agency was "locked in and ready to ship its rules" for crypto markets.

## What does a prerule mean for the timeline?

OIRA review is the current gate. Executive Order 12866 gives the White House office 10 working days to review a preliminary action like this, against 90 days for a proposed or final rule. Ten working days from September 17 lands around October 1, and the CFTC cannot publish anything until OIRA finishes or waives the review.

What comes after is the long road. The text would need to become a proposed rule, survive a Commission vote, get printed in the Federal Register, and take public comments. Then a second CFTC vote before anything binds anyone. Even with a smooth run, a finished rule is months away.

Cloture is the vote that ends debate in the Senate so a bill can move forward. Failing it on a motion to proceed means the chamber never formally took up the bill.

## Why is the CFTC pushing ahead without Congress?

The Senate killed the bill first. On September 15, the chamber voted 49-50 against cloture on a motion to proceed to H.R. 3633, the Digital Asset Market CLARITY Act, putting it 11 votes short of the 60 needed. All 49 yes votes came from Republicans. Josh Hawley and Jerry Moran voted no, and Thom Tillis switched his vote at the last minute, a procedural move that preserves the option to bring the bill back up this Congress.

Selig had already teed up the fallback. On August 20 he told CFTC staff to work up crypto rules under the commission's existing authority, and he repeated that pledge the day after the vote. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had earlier floated agency rulemaking as the route if legislation stalled.

The two agencies have kept moving on other fronts this week. The SEC granted an "innovation exemption" that lets qualifying venues trade tokenized US stocks on blockchain without registering as national exchanges. The CFTC issued no-action relief that spares some crypto wallet apps from registering as introducing brokers.

A no-action letter is a staff commitment not to bring an enforcement action against a specific activity; it is a promise, not a rule.

## Why doesn't rulemaking replace the CLARITY Act?

Because an agency rule can be reversed. A statute stays on the books across administrations; a regulation written by one White House can be torn up by the next or struck down in court. That is the gap Bessent and Selig keep walking around.

The CFTC also has to ground whatever it writes in powers Congress already gave it. The agency runs the US derivatives market: futures, swaps, and the products wrapped around them. Its reach over spot commodity markets is narrower. The legal basis RIN 3038-AF80 leans on matters more than its title, and firms cannot know what is covered until the actual text surfaces.

The market is pricing the bill like this. Polymarket odds of the CLARITY Act becoming law in 2026 sat at 7.9% at the end of the week, down from 19.5% on Tuesday morning before the vote.

## What to watch next

- Whether OIRA wraps its review inside the roughly 10 working day window, which lands near October 1.
- What the proposed text covers: which transactions, which venues, which intermediaries, and which existing law justifies each part.
- Whether senators revive the bill before the October recess. Seven Democratic senators have said talks will continue after the vote, though lead Republican negotiator Cynthia Lummis called the bill all but dead this year.

## Bottom line

The CFTC moved first after the CLARITY Act collapsed, filing this rulemaking with the White House within days of the vote. But it filed a prerule, the softest official step in federal rulemaking, and the working text is still locked away. An agency can build a bridge with the authority it already has, yet only Congress can write a law that survives the next election. Watch the OIRA timeline and the first public draft of the proposal; those will tell you whether this becomes a real framework or just a paper trail.
