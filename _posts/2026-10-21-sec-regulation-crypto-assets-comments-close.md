The SEC's public comment period on Regulation Crypto Assets closed October 20. More than 100 letters are sitting in the file, and each one is a pitch for how the first US offering rule written for crypto tokens should read.

This is the rule that decides how projects raise money in the United States without registering every sale as a securities offering. The comments now go to SEC staff, who can revise the proposal, water it down, or start over. Nothing is final until the agency adopts text.

## What is Regulation Crypto Assets?

Regulation Crypto Assets is a proposed SEC rule that would create a tailored offering regime for certain crypto token sales. The SEC proposed it on August 18, and the Federal Register published it on August 21 with comments due October 20.

The proposal carries two exemptions from Securities Act registration. A startup exemption would permit offerings of up to $5 million across a four-year period, backed by narrative disclosures instead of a full registration statement. A fundraising exemption, modeled on Regulation A, would run two tiers and top out at $75 million per 12-month period, with financial statements and ongoing reporting required at that level. Antifraud and antimanipulation rules still apply to both.

It also includes a conditional safe harbor. Meet the conditions, and a crypto asset is deemed not to be subject to the "investment contract" definition in US securities law. A final subpart would define "qualified purchaser" broadly enough to preempt state-level registration for covered offerings and certain secondary trades.

## What did the industry ask to change?

The Hyperliquid Policy Center told the SEC to adopt the rule but fix two pieces. The first is airdrops. The center asked the SEC to codify language saying tokens handed out for nothing, with no investment of money in exchange, are not securities. The second is the safe harbor itself, which the center argues "requires a certification that many projects cannot make."

The Alternative Investment Management Association went at disclosure. Its letter asked issuers to distinguish utility or economic exposure from ownership, redemption, cash-flow, and contractual rights, and to treat genuinely unavailable information differently in filings.

Wall Street weighed in through SIFMA, the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association. Its argument is about timing. The separate innovation exemption, which opened the door to onchain trading of tokenized stocks, went into effect in September as a five-year relief. "The five-year term of the relief heightens the importance of resolving these questions at the outset, since early design choices will shape market practice well before any permanent framework is adopted," SIFMA wrote.

## What happens next?

The SEC has not adopted a final rule and has not published a timeline for one. Staff will now work through the comment file, and the final rule can look quite different from the August proposal.

Two more rulemakings keep the calendar full. A pair of market-structure proposals, Regulation Crypto Asset Transactions and Regulation Crypto Asset Markets, keeps comments open until December 14. And Congress is out of the picture for now: the CLARITY market structure bill failed a Senate procedural vote 49-50 on September 15, which is exactly why SEC and CFTC rulemaking suddenly carries so much weight.

What to watch if you hold tokens: the airdrop language decides whether assets you received for free get treated as securities under US law. The safe harbor's certification requirement decides how many projects can actually use it. And the $5 million and $75 million caps decide who can fundraise in the open.

## Bottom line

The comment file on Regulation Crypto Assets is closed with more than 100 letters in it, and the SEC now decides how much of the industry's wish list becomes the rule. Watch the airdrop language, the safe-harbor certification, and the December 14 deadline on the market-structure pair. Until the agency adopts text, the August proposal is still just a proposal.
