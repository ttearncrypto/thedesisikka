For most of the past decade, the conversation about banks and blockchains has followed a familiar script. A large institution announces a pilot. The pilot runs on a private network nobody outside the consortium can touch. Two years later the press release quietly disappears from the website.

On September 16, 2026, Circle Internet Group tried something structurally different. The company behind USDC switched on the public mainnet of Arc, a Layer 1 blockchain it calls an "Economic Operating System" for the internet. What makes the launch unusual is the list of names running the validators.

BlackRock. The Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation. Visa. Mastercard. ICE, the company that owns the New York Stock Exchange. Standard Chartered. MoneyGram. SBI Group. Galaxy. Sumitomo Corporation. Worldpay, now part of Global Payments.

These firms form the founding validator cohort, according to Circle. The institutions that clear global securities, operate payment rails and manage global capital are running Arc as validators, not signing up as customers. That is a meaningful shift in what "institutional adoption" has meant until now.

## What exactly is Arc?

Arc is an open, EVM-compatible Layer 1 blockchain purpose-built for three things: financial markets, real-time money movement, and what Circle calls agentic economic activity (transactions initiated and settled by AI agents rather than humans clicking buttons).

The network is launched by Arc Network Services LLC and operated by a permissioned validator set. That phrase matters and we will come back to it, because it is simultaneously Arc's biggest selling point to banks and its most obvious criticism from the crypto-native community.

Circle lays out six design decisions that separate Arc from general-purpose chains.

### 1. Gas paid in dollars

On Arc, transaction fees are denominated in USDC. There is no requirement to hold a volatile native asset just to move money.

If you have ever tried to explain to a finance team why they need to keep a floating balance of ETH in a treasury wallet purely to pay for gas, you already understand why this is a bigger deal than it sounds. Volatile gas is an accounting problem, a treasury problem, and an audit problem all at once. Pricing fees in a dollar-denominated stablecoin removes an entire category of internal objections.

### 2. Sub-second finality

Arc offers [deterministic settlement]({{ '/pages/learn/glossary/' | relative_url }}) with sub-second finality. Circle's framing is blunt: final means final.

Probabilistic finality, where a transaction becomes progressively less likely to be reversed as blocks pile on top of it, is a genuinely awkward fit for regulated finance. Settlement systems are built around a moment where legal title transfers and cannot be undone. Deterministic consensus gives you that moment.

### 3. Opt-in privacy with view keys

Arc's design includes confidential transactions and balances paired with view keys, so that a party can grant selective visibility to an auditor, a regulator, or a counterparty without exposing their activity to the entire public.

Circle notes this capability is still in development for network-wide release, which is worth flagging clearly rather than treating it as shipped.

### 4. A home for issuers and tokenized assets

Arc is designed as native territory for USDC, EURC, and tokenized real-world assets. Circle StableFX provides 24/7 cross-currency settlement across a growing basket of local stablecoins.

### 5. Built from genesis for AI agents

Circle's claim here is that Arc is the first blockchain designed from the ground up with AI agents as first-class economic participants rather than an afterthought bolted on later.

### 6. Institutional-grade security

Arc supports post-quantum signatures today, with broader post-quantum protections described as in development. Consensus is built to financial market standards.

Post-quantum readiness rarely makes headlines, but for institutions writing 10 and 20 year infrastructure plans, "we will deal with quantum later" is not an acceptable answer in a procurement review.

## The agentic economy angle

This is the part of the announcement that will get argued about most. Read it carefully.

Circle's argument is that AI agents are already doing economic work: executing trades, routing liquidity, managing payments, and executing contracts on behalf of people and businesses. The company points to data indicating that USDC represents 98.8% of agent-driven transaction volume, and says that since Circle Agent Stack launched in May 2026, the large majority of agent-to-agent payments settling over the x402 standard have used USDC.

Arc ships with three components aimed at this:

- **Circle Agent Stack**, which provides policy-controlled Agent Wallets, Nanopayments powered by Circle Gateway, and an emerging Agent Marketplace.
- **Arc Portal**, a consumer-facing surface where a person can fund an agent wallet, set spending limits, and delegate onchain tasks while retaining visibility and control.
- **AgentVM**, described as in design, which aims to let agents process sensitive data in a protected environment while Arc keeps an immutable record that lets applications verify the provenance of a result without exposing the underlying data.

### Why agents need a different payment rail

Human payments are chunky and infrequent. You buy a coffee, you pay rent, you settle an invoice. Agent payments are the opposite: potentially thousands of micro-settlements per hour for API calls, data queries, compute time, or model inference.

Card networks are structurally wrong for that shape of traffic because interchange economics collapse below a certain ticket size. Traditional bank rails close on weekends. Volatile-gas blockchains introduce cost uncertainty into every single call.

Whether or not you believe the agentic economy arrives on Circle's timeline, the infrastructure gap it describes is real.

## The ARC token

This is where a lot of coverage will get sloppy. Be precise about what was and was not announced.

Circle completed a genesis mint of the ARC token in the United States, creating the full initial supply of 10 billion tokens. Circle says this makes it the first publicly traded company to mint a network token for a new Layer 1 blockchain.

What ARC is designed to be: a digital commodity acting as the native coordination mechanism for security, utility, and governance on the network.

What ARC is not, at least today:
- It is **not** the gas token. Network fees remain payable in USDC.
- The mint is explicitly described as **not a commitment** to publicly launch ARC.
- It is framed as a technical milestone tied to a possible future transition from Proof of Authority toward Proof of Stake, which Circle says the network will explore in **2027**.

If you see a post claiming an ARC airdrop is confirmed or that ARC is trading, treat it as unverified until Circle says otherwise. A genesis mint and a token launch are different events, and the gap between them is where scams live.

**Practical advice:** be extremely cautious of any token claiming to be ARC on secondary markets right now, any site offering an "ARC claim" connected to [wallet approvals]({{ '/pages/learn/wallet-and-security/' | relative_url }}), and any Telegram or X account promising early allocation. New chain launches attract impersonation attacks within hours.

## Developer experience

Arc is fully EVM compatible, so existing Solidity contracts and standard Ethereum tooling work without a rewrite. That decision alone removes most of the switching cost that has sunk alternative virtual machines.

Circle reports the testnet processed more than 700 million transactions in under a year, and that the developer community includes more than 75,000 active Arc House members and 10,000 Architect ambassadors who have built more than 1,200 projects.

Two new tools shipped with mainnet:

### Arc Studio

An onchain coding agent that generates an application from an idea, including smart contracts, application logic, and deployment-ready code for the network.

### Arc App Kits

A unified SDK covering core onchain fund flows, letting developers add payments, swaps, onramps, and yield without assembling each piece separately.

Together with Arc Portal, Circle now ships entry points for end users, builders and enterprise teams from one stack.

## StableFX and the multi-currency story

One of the more underrated pieces here is Circle StableFX, which enables 24/7 programmable foreign exchange with near-instant, low-cost settlement.

The list of local stablecoins active or onboarding includes USDC, EURC, AUDD, AUDF, BRLA, CADD, CHFAU, EURAU, GBPA, JPYC, KRW1, MXNB, QCAD, SEKAU, TRYB, wARS, wBRL, wCLP, wCOP, wMXN, wPEN, and ZARU.

Circle Payments Network is integrated natively into Arc, with cross-border movement priced at a fraction of a cent and settling in near real time.

### Why this matters for India and emerging markets

Global FX still runs on a Monday to Friday schedule with correspondent banking hops that add days and basis points. [Remittance corridors]({{ '/pages/learn/p2p-and-onramps/' | relative_url }}) into South Asia, Latin America, and Africa absorb that cost directly.

A 24/7 settlement layer with sub-cent fees is a structural threat to that cost base. Note carefully, though, that no INR-pegged stablecoin appears on the StableFX list at launch. India's regulatory position on stablecoins and rupee-denominated tokens remains restrictive, and the country's own digital rupee pilot sits under Reserve Bank of India control rather than private issuance. Indian users should not read this launch as a change in local rules.

If you are in India and considering any of this, start with the [tax position]({{ '/pages/learn/bitcoin-tax-guide-india/' | relative_url }}) before the technology. Virtual digital asset gains are taxed at a flat 30% with 1% TDS on transfers, and stablecoin transactions are not exempt from that framework.

## The permissioned validator debate

Here is the honest tension at the centre of Arc, and any coverage that skips it is not doing its job.

Arc is a public blockchain with a permissioned validator set. Anyone can read the chain, deploy contracts, and transact. Not just anyone can validate.

**The case for it:** banks cannot use infrastructure where the entity ordering transactions is unknown and unaccountable. A defined governance perimeter and a known validator set are what make Arc procurement-approvable at institutions like State Street or HSBC. Circle also points to the regulatory clarity of the GENIUS Act as part of what makes bank participation viable.

**The case against it:** credible neutrality in blockchains has historically come from permissionless validation. A network whose validators are a dozen of the largest financial institutions on earth inherits the censorship characteristics of those institutions. If a transaction is unwelcome to that cohort, the assumption that it will be processed anyway is weaker than on Ethereum.

Circle's stated answer is the roadmap: a possible move from Proof of Authority toward Proof of Stake in 2027, with the ARC token as the coordination mechanism. Until that happens, the tradeoff is what it is, and prospective users should evaluate Arc on its own terms.

## The road ahead

Circle's forward roadmap centres on what it calls Network Sectors, purpose-built environments for different classes of financial activity:

| Sector | Purpose | Stated target |
|---|---|---|
| **Privacy Sector** | Opt-in confidential transactions | In development |
| **Payment Sector** | Low-cost payments at scale | More than 100,000 transactions per second |
| **Agent Sector** | Verifiable agent identity and auditable records | In development |

Alongside this sit the Proof of Stake exploration and broader post-quantum protections.

Treat all of these as stated intentions. Circle's own disclaimer notes that Arc features may be modified, delayed, or cancelled at any time without notice. That is boilerplate, but it is accurate boilerplate.

## What this means for the wider market

A few observations, treated as analysis.

**Circle is moving up the stack.** For most of its history Circle earned primarily from reserve income on USDC. Owning the settlement layer, the payments network, the FX layer, the agent stack, and the developer tooling changes the shape of that business. Whether it changes the economics is a question for CRCL shareholders and the next few earnings calls.

**Layer 1 competition is fragmenting by purpose.** BlackRock's global head of digital assets framed the trend as networks increasingly being designed around specific markets and use cases. That is a notable framing from a firm with no obligation to flatter Circle.

**Stablecoin infrastructure is consolidating around regulated issuers.** The GENIUS Act created a compliance path that favours issuers who can meet it. Arc is what building on top of that clarity looks like.

**Execution risk remains the story.** Launching with 100 institutional names on a slide is not the same as processing institutional volume. The metric to watch over the next two quarters is not partner count. It is settled value, active addresses that are not airdrop farmers, and whether the banks listed move from "exploring" to "in production."

## Bottom line

Strip away the launch-day language and Arc is a bet on a specific claim: that the next decade of finance needs a settlement layer that never closes, settles instantly, prices fees in dollars, and is trusted enough that a custodian bank will run a node on it.

Circle has assembled a genuinely serious coalition to test that claim. DTCC and ICE are not names that appear on speculative crypto launches. The wallet and exchange coverage means the network is reachable from day one. EVM compatibility means developers do not have to learn anything new.

What remains unproven is everything that matters after the press cycle: whether volume follows, whether the privacy layer ships as described, whether the Proof of Stake transition happens, and whether a permissioned validator set holds up as a credible foundation for an open financial system.

Those questions get answered in throughput data over the coming quarters, not in announcements. We will be watching the numbers.
