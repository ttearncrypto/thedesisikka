# What human writers do instead

## Rhythm (burstiness)
Mix sentence lengths on purpose. A 4 word sentence next to a 30 word one. Some paragraphs one sentence, some four. Target: sentence length standard deviation of 7+ words across the article. Do not fake it with fragments everywhere; that is its own tell.

## Specifics beat adjectives
Swap every abstract claim for a concrete one: a number with its source, a named tool, a date, a place, a price, a real example from the research. Example: "Calculators disagree" becomes "Some calculators subtract 22 from totals above 22, others add the digits" (a checkable fact).

## Take a position
Say what you think is overrated, wrong, or missing in competitor content, and why. Readers trust a writer who disagrees with someone. Keep it fair and sourced.

## Uneven attention
Go deep where the reader's real question is. Be brief or skip where the topic is thin. Humans get bored and it shows; that is fine.

## Plain words
use, not utilize. show, not demonstrate. then, not subsequently. help, not facilitate. Contractions are normal (don't, it's, you'll) throughout, not only in "friendly" parts.

## Transitions
Prefer none, or ordinary ones: "So", "But", "Still", "That said", "Here's the catch". Starting a sentence with And or But is fine.

## Humour that works
Observational and specific to the niche. Mild self-awareness. One aside in parentheses. Understatement. Never a joke that needs a fake fact.

## Voice variety
Mix: a direct instruction, a short opinion, a fact with a source, a question the reader is actually asking, an aside. Do not repeat the same paragraph shape three times in a row.

## Real experience
If the user supplied first-hand material, put it early and use exact details (numbers, timeframes, what went wrong). If not, write without anecdotes and suggest spots to the user outside the article. Never invent one. A fabricated anecdote is worse than none: it breaks reader trust and Google's quality guidelines on accuracy.

## Endings
End on something useful: a next step, a specific recommendation, a one-line opinion. No recap heading.

## Opening types (rotate; never reuse the last one used for this user)
1. A concrete fact or number from a primary source, stated flatly, no drama.
2. The reader's exact situation in one plain sentence ("You have ₹20,000 saved and a weekend free.").
3. A first-hand detail from the user's own material.
4. A direct answer to the query in the first sentence, then the condition that changes it.
5. A short, specific observation from the research (something odd you found on page one of Google).
Banned openers: announcing honesty, attacking "most guides", "If you're reading this...", "Let's face it".

## Using data like a person
- Cite the number, then add one line of context a reader would ask about (year, sample size, country).
- Include at least one fact that cuts against your main point, and deal with it.
- Prefer data that matches the target market (RBI, NSO, NASSCOM for India; BLS, Census for US).

## Ending types (rotate)
A specific next action with a number or deadline; a single concrete recommendation for one reader type; a short note on what you'd check first. No moral, no "focus on X instead" lesson, no summary.
