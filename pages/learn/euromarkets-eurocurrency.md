The Euromarket has nothing to do with the European Union and nothing to do with the euro. It is the market where banks lend dollars to each other outside the United States, and it holds one of the largest pools of money on the planet.

This guide explains what it is, how it grew out of the Cold War, what the instruments are, who runs it now, and where the risks sit.

## The short version

A eurocurrency is any currency deposited or borrowed in a bank outside the country that issues it. A Eurodollar is US dollars held in a bank outside the US. A Euroyen is yen held outside Japan.

That is the whole trick. Take a currency, move the banking outside its home jurisdiction, and you have something the issuer's regulators control less tightly. The absence of domestic reserve requirements, capital controls and interest rate caps makes offshore dollar funding cheaper for some borrowers and more profitable for some lenders.

The "Euro-" prefix is historical. The market started in Europe, mostly London, and the label stuck even though the business now runs everywhere.

## How it started

Three separate problems lined up and created the market.

**Soviet dollars.** Soviet and Eastern Bloc governments held reserves in US banks and did not want them frozen during a Cold War confrontation. So they parked dollars with European banks, mostly London, from the mid-1950s onward. Catherine Schenk's research on the history of London puts the birth of the Eurodollar market there between 1955 and 1963. Those deposits became the first pool of offshore dollars.

**Regulation Q.** US law capped the interest that banks could pay on deposits. A dollar deposit in New York paid less than the same dollar in London, so the business moved offshore where the ceiling did not apply.

**Capital controls.** Washington ran balance-of-payments controls between 1963 and 1974, including the Interest Equalisation Tax from 1961. Firms looking to park dollars or borrow them outside the US found it easier offshore.

London wanted the business. British banks had the machinery, the client base and the desperation to keep their international position after the empire faded, and they took the deposits.

## What the market became

Oil shocks did the rest. The 1973-74 and 1979-80 price rises left oil exporters with huge surpluses, and those petrodollars went straight into the Euromarket. Banks recycled them into loans to oil importers, sovereign governments and corporations.

By the 1980s, Eurodollar futures had overtaken Treasury bill futures as the hedging instrument of choice at the short end of the dollar yield curve. The market was no longer a corner of London. It had its own instruments, its own benchmarks and its own risk.

## The instruments

**Eurocurrency deposits.** Time deposits in a currency that is not the bank's local currency. Fixed rate, fixed term, usually non-negotiable, usually unsecured. USD deposits pay interest on a 360-day year, sterling deposits on 365.

**Eurocurrency loans.** The same idea on the lending side. Fixed rate, floating rate tied to a benchmark, or syndicated across a group of lenders. Floating loans now price off SOFR for dollars, SONIA for sterling, €STR for euros, TONAR for yen and SARON for francs.

**Euronotes.** Short and medium-term negotiable paper. Euro Commercial Paper runs from one month to a year. Euro Medium-Term Notes go from one to five years. Euro CDs are negotiable bank deposits. Issuers pick the timing and size, so the market is flexible.

**Eurobonds.** Long-term debt in a currency other than the issuer's home currency, usually 5 to 30 years, often listed in London or Luxembourg. Straight, floating-rate, convertible, zero-coupon and dual-currency structures all exist here. Minimum denominations run around $5,000.

**Euro-equities.** Shares issued simultaneously across several national markets outside the company's home country, usually through an international syndicate. Global Depositary Receipts are the classic form.

**Derivatives.** Futures on Eurodollar rates, options, cross-currency swaps and forward rate agreements. When interbank lending dries up, these are where banks try to source funding.

## How the market is put together

**Primary market.** Where new paper is issued. Investment banks underwrite, meaning they guarantee the purchase, then price and allocate to investors.

**Secondary market.** Where that paper changes hands. Dealers and market makers quote continuous prices, brokers match buyers and sellers, and electronic platforms now handle a large share of price discovery.

**Interbank market.** Where banks lend to each other, mostly overnight to one year. This is the plumbing. Money market desks manage the short-term liquidity, and credit lines are standing arrangements between counterparties.

## Why anyone uses it

**Cheaper funding.** No reserve requirement on the deposit side and lighter supervision means offshore rates can sit above onshore rates for the same currency. Corporates issuing Eurobonds have sometimes paid less than in their home market.

**Maturity and currency matching.** A company earning dollars can borrow dollars. That removes the currency mismatch that sank a lot of 1990s emerging market debt.

**Speed.** Euro notes can be issued in days, not the weeks a domestic bond takes. That matters when you need cash now.

**Privacy.** Disclosure rules offshore are looser than in many home markets, which is attractive and also a source of regret after a few scandals.

**Arbitrage.** Where onshore and offshore rates diverge, the gap gets traded. That arbitrage is what keeps the two rates from drifting too far apart.

## The scale, and who runs it now

The Bank for International Settlements runs the numbers, and its March 2026 Quarterly Review put a spotlight on offshore activity. Some of the findings:

- Dollar credit to non-bank borrowers outside the US was **$14.3 trillion at the end of 2025**, per BIS data. Steve Hanke put offshore USD deposits at a record **$14.5 trillion** in late April 2026, up about 220% since 2000, and estimated the eurodollar market itself at around **$16 trillion**.
- Separate BIS estimates put off-balance-sheet dollar obligations from FX swaps, forwards and currency swaps at **$26 trillion** for non-banks outside the US, as of end-June 2022.
- **Cross-border financial centres** are the engine. These are places whose business is mostly with non-residents: the Cayman Islands, Ireland, Luxembourg, Bermuda and the Bahamas. Their external positions grew from 12% of the global total in 1980 to nearly 30% in 2023.
- Those centres now hold **almost $60 trillion** in external positions, **20 times their combined GDP**, which accounts for just 2.8% of global economic activity.
- Nearly a third of international debt securities, about **$11 trillion**, are issued by affiliates outside the issuer's home country.
- Banks' offshore affiliates account for **more than 40% of global cross-border bank lending**.
- A quarter of the cross-border bank credit going to Brazil comes from Brazilian bank offices sitting in the Cayman Islands and the Bahamas.

One BIS example sticks: Vale, the Brazilian mining company, issues bonds through a special purpose vehicle domiciled in the Cayman Islands. The loan gets recorded twice, once against the centre where the vehicle sits and once against Brazil. Residence-based statistics make the exposure hard to see. Nationality-based BIS data makes it visible.

That is the core point about the Euromarket. It is the plumbing that decides where credit actually flows, and light-touch regulation is part of why those flows land where they do.

## The regulation, and the gaps

The Euromarket started with almost no direct supervision. That has changed, but slowly and unevenly.

**Basel standards.** The Basel Committee's capital, liquidity and risk rules apply to banks wherever they book their business, which means much of the Euromarket is now subject to them in substance.

**After 2008.** The crisis exposed what happened when nobody was counting. Transparency rules tightened, counterparty risk got more attention, and the market infrastructure improved. The BIS estimated the global dollar funding shortfall at $2 trillion to $6 trillion during the crunch.

**The LIBOR handover.** Most USD LIBOR settings ended on December 31, 2021, with the remainder going by June 30, 2023. The replacements are SOFR, SONIA, €STR, TONAR and SARON. The switch was harder than expected, because USD LIBOR was global and the replacement rates are mostly domestic in character.

## The risks

**Credit risk.** The borrower does not pay. Managed with analysis, diversification and collateral.

**Currency risk.** You borrowed dollars and earn rupees, or the reverse. Hedged with forwards, futures, options and swaps.

**Interest rate risk.** Rates move against you. Managed with maturity matching, derivatives or by paying floating.

**Liquidity risk.** You cannot trade at the price you expected. This is the dangerous one. In 2008 the market stopped lending and every other risk became a crisis at once.

**Country and political risk.** A jurisdiction changes its rules, freezes assets or changes its government.

**Systemic risk.** The pieces are linked. One institution failing can pull on the rest. The 2008 crisis is the reference case.

## Three case studies worth knowing

**The Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s.** Petrodollar recycling pushed huge amounts of dollar debt onto Latin American governments. When US rates rose and commodity prices fell, the math broke. Defaults, restructurings and a lost decade followed. The Brady Plan eventually converted bank loans into tradable bonds with partial guarantees. The lesson is that foreign currency debt taken on at the wrong moment is the same debt with a trigger attached.

**The Asian financial crisis of 1997 and 1998.** Short-term foreign currency borrowing, often denominated in dollars, funded domestic assets that earned in local currency. When the Thai baht and then the others devalued, the debt burden multiplied. The lesson is a currency mismatch on top of a maturity mismatch.

**The global financial crisis of 2008.** Structured products and shadow banking grew on top of offshore dollar funding. Interbank lending dried up. Major institutions failed and governments stepped in. Basel III, Dodd-Frank and a wave of clearing and transparency rules came after.

## Where it is heading

**Electronification.** Voice broking and faxes gave way to electronic platforms, and the share of electronically executed trades keeps climbing.

**Tokenised money.** Stablecoin supply reached about **$317 billion by early 2026**, up from under $2 billion in early 2020 and past $280 billion by late 2025. Set that beside $14.3 trillion of offshore dollar credit and it is still small, but it is a retail-shaped dollar liability sitting outside the US banking system, and it is growing at a pace the interbank market cannot match.

**De-dollarisation.** Countries with dollar-heavy trade surpluses keep looking for alternatives. Attempts so far have produced settlement systems and trade-finance units rather than a genuine substitute, and the dollar's share of reserves has fallen from its 2001 high without a successor emerging.

**Regulatory divergence.** Different jurisdictions, different rules, more complexity for everyone who operates across them.

## Why this matters if you hold dollars

If you buy crypto with Indian rupees, the offshore dollar market is still in your corner of the world. US monetary policy moves dollar rates, which move offshore dollar rates, which feed into global liquidity. Cheap offshore funding supports risk assets. Tight funding does the opposite.

That is the channel that explains why crypto prices sometimes move on a Fed statement that has nothing to do with crypto.
